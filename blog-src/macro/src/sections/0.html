Section 0

Preparation: Candidate Constraints and the Optimizer

Before we explore what weight constraints change, let's define the training problem and choose an optimizer.

Let W∈Rp×n be a weight matrix. Its input dimension is n, and its output dimension is p. We minimize the training loss L(W), while keeping W in a constraint set M:

minW∈ML(W).

We start with two ways to fix matrix scale. The Frobenius norm is the square root of the sum of squared entries. The spectral norm is the largest singular value. It measures the largest factor by which the matrix can stretch an input vector.

The two constraint sets are

MF(R)={W:‖W‖F=R},MS(R)={W:‖W‖2=R}.

The radius R>0 sets the required norm. Both constraints leave the individual entries free to change.1

Both constraints act on the whole matrix. Later, we will ask whether each row or column needs its own norm constraint.

To train under these constraints, we propose the Msign-Aligned Constrained Riemannian Optimizer (MACRO). Its two variants are MACRO-fro and MACRO-spec.

Why not simply use MuonH or SSO? We want a practical testbed with a convergence guarantee. MuonH normalizes weights and updates, without an explicit tangent-space projection. SSO solves a constrained direction problem through an inner iterative search.45

MACRO builds on the MCSD approach to balance manifold optimization with training cost. It uses a single-loop update, with a convergence guarantee under the paper's assumptions. Here is how the pieces fit together.61

How does MACRO update the weights?

At step t, MACRO forms momentum Mt from the current gradient and previous momentum. It then projects Mt onto the tangent space at Wt, giving Φt. This explicit projection distinguishes MACRO from MuonH and is central to our convergence analysis.14

MACRO then follows the spectral steepest-descent framework to compute the update direction:

Ot=msign(Φt).

For a full-rank matrix, this operation keeps the singular vectors and sets the singular values to one.

Next, we scale this direction with the constraint radius:

∇~t=ROt‖Ot‖M+ε.

Here, ‖⋅‖M is the Frobenius norm for MACRO-fro and the spectral norm for MACRO-spec.

The constraint fixes the weight scale. This rescaling controls the proposed update size relative to it. We return to this update-to-weight ratio in Section 3, where small updates can disappear during rounding.1

We then take a step with learning rate ηt. A retraction returns the candidate weights to the constraint set:

Wt+1=PM(Wt−ηt∇~t).

How do we choose the radius?

We now have a constraint and an update rule. But how large should the constraint be?

The same radius can mean different things for different matrices. The Frobenius norm combines squared values from all entries. The spectral norm measures the largest stretch along an input direction. So we choose the radius from the layer's effect on its inputs, not from the norm value alone.

Our aim is to control activation scale as layer widths change. This shares a goal with width-aware parameterizations such as μP.7

Consider the linear layer Y=XW0⊤. Let rX and rY be the RMS values of its inputs and outputs. We choose dimension-dependent radii and leave a separate multiplier r>0 for tuning. For normalized Gaussian initialization, the rules are:2

Frobenius

Rfro=rp

Expected squared output RMS:

EG[rY2∣X]=r2rX2

Spectral

Rspec=rp/n

Output RMS bound:

rY=rrXwith high probability

For the assumptions and probability bounds, open Proposition 1 below. The formulas account for the input dimension n and output dimension p. The multiplier r is independent of these dimensions. Does this extra hyperparameter make constraints harder to use? In Section 1, we show that training is robust to changes in this multiplier when RMSNorm and weight constraints work together.

Proposition 1: Radius selection at initialization

Let T,n,p≥1, r>0, and X∈RT×n. Define

rX=‖X‖FTn,rY=‖XW0⊤‖FTp.

Let G∈Rp×n have independent standard normal entries. The matrix G is independent of X.

Frobenius constraint. Set

W0=rpG‖G‖F.

Then

EG[rY2∣X]=r2rX2.

Spectral constraint. Set

W0=rpnG‖G‖2.

The output satisfies the deterministic bound

rY≤rrX.

For any ε∈(0,1) and a>0,

rY≥r1−εn/p+1+arX

with conditional probability at least

1−exp(−pε2/4)−exp(−a2p/2).

The probability is over G, for a fixed X.

What if we constrain each row or column?

Earlier, we fixed one norm for the whole matrix. Would finer control help?

The Frobenius sphere fixes the total squared norm. It does not fix how that total is divided among rows or columns. Oblique manifolds add constraints at this finer level. An output-oblique constraint gives every row the same fixed norm. An input-oblique constraint does the same for columns.3

We match each oblique variant to the same total Frobenius norm as MACRO-fro. This keeps the overall scale fixed while changing how it is shared.

In these tests, neither oblique variant improves validation loss over the matched Frobenius sphere. We therefore focus on the Frobenius and spectral spheres in the following sections.3
